Module 1 • Core Concepts

Introduction to Statistical Thinking & Data Basics

Master the core distinction between raw data and actionable information, learn the fundamental role of statistical methodology, and explore the 4 levels of measurement scales.

Statistical Thinking & Data vs Info

Statistical Thinking is a decision-making philosophy based on understanding, controlling, and describing variation in data.

Data: Raw, unorganized facts, symbols, or numerical measurements (e.g., raw numbers like 70, 40, 15).
Information: Processed, structured, and interpreted data that provides meaningful context (e.g., Average score = 38.2%).

Objectives of Statistics

Statistics provides mathematical procedures to convert raw facts into actionable insights.

  • Summarize Data: Condense large datasets into intuitive metrics.
  • Measure Variability: Quantify uncertainty and spread.
  • Infer Patterns: Draw general conclusions about populations from samples.

The 4 Scales of Measurement

Data variables are categorized into four hierarchical measurement scales. Higher levels retain the properties of lower levels with additional mathematical capabilities.

1. Nominal Lowest

Labels, categories, or names only. No order or numerical meaning.

Examples: Hair Color, Gender, Eye Color, Zip Code, Marital Status.
2. Ordinal Ordered

Ordered ranks or categories, but intervals between values are not uniform.

Examples: Customer Satisfaction (Poor, Fair, Good), Class Rank, Education Level.
3. Interval Equal Intervals

Meaningful distance between values, but no true/absolute zero point.

Examples: Temperature (°C / °F), SAT Score, Calendar Year.
4. Ratio Absolute Zero

Ordered with equal intervals AND a true absolute zero (absence of attribute).

Examples: Height, Weight, Salary, Distance, Elapsed Time.

Lesson 1 Practice Exercises

Exercise 1.1 Scale Identification

Classify each of the following variables into their corresponding scale of measurement:
a) Temperature in Celsius, b) SAT Exam Score, c) Student Height (cm), d) Customer Satisfaction rating (Low, Medium, High), e) Calendar Year (2024).

Solution & Reasoning:

  • Temperature in Celsius: Interval Scale (0°C does not mean complete absence of heat).
  • SAT Exam Score: Interval Scale (Scores do not start at a true absolute zero).
  • Student Height (cm): Ratio Scale (0 cm represents absolute zero height; 180cm is twice as tall as 90cm).
  • Customer Satisfaction: Ordinal Scale (Ordered hierarchy, but distance between categories is non-quantifiable).
  • Calendar Year: Interval Scale (Year 0 is arbitrary and does not mark the beginning of time).
Exercise 1.2 Conceptual Reasoning

Why is Temperature measured in Kelvin considered a Ratio scale, whereas Temperature measured in Celsius is an Interval scale?

Solution:

Celsius uses an arbitrary zero point (the freezing point of water), meaning 0°C does not indicate an absence of thermal energy. Consequently, 40°C is not "twice as hot" as 20°C.

Kelvin has a true absolute zero (0 K = absolute zero, where molecular motion stops). Thus, 200 K represents twice the thermal kinetic energy of 100 K, fulfilling the requirement for a ratio scale.

Module 2 • Data Structuring

Data Organization & Visualizations

Learn how to transform raw chaotic datasets into Data Arrays, construct Stem-and-Leaf Plots (including comparative back-to-back plots), and build frequency distributions.

1. Data Array & Range

A Data Array is an arrangement of raw numerical observations in ascending or descending order.

Primary Benefit: Allows immediate identification of maximum and minimum values, facilitating quick calculation of the range.

Range = Highest Value (H) - Lowest Value (L)

2. Stem-and-Leaf Plots

A display tool that splits numbers into a Stem (leading digit/s) and a Leaf (trailing trailing digit).

Comparative / Back-to-Back Plots: Used to compare two distributions side-by-side using a single central stem.

Exercise 2.1 Data Array & Range

Given the following raw dataset representing exam scores: [45, 12, 70, 30, 55, 20, 65, 15], construct a Data Array and determine the Range.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Module 3 • Central Tendency

Descriptive Statistics - Measures of Central Tendency

Differentiate between Descriptive and Inferential statistics, and learn how to compute and interpret the Mean, Median, and Mode.

Descriptive Statistics

Methods used to organize, summarize, and present data in an informative way (e.g., calculating average class performance).

Inferential Statistics

Methods used to estimate or draw conclusions about a larger population based on sample data (e.g., hypothesis testing, polling).

The Three Main Averages

Arithmetic Mean (x̄)

Sum of all observations divided by the total number of observations (N).

x̄ = ( Σ xᵢ ) / N

Note: Sensitive to extreme outliers.

Median (M)

The middle observation when data is arranged in order.

Position = (N + 1) / 2

Note: Robust against outliers.

Mode

The most frequently occurring value in the dataset.

Uni / Bi / Multi-Modal

Note: Datasets can have 0, 1, or multiple modes.

Module 4 • Data Spread & Consistency

Descriptive Statistics - Measures of Dispersion

Understand how data spreads around central values using Range, Variance, Standard Deviation, and the Coefficient of Variation (CV) for consistency testing.

Variance & Standard Deviation

Measures average squared deviation around the mean.

Sample Variance (s²) = Σ(xᵢ - x̄)² / (N - 1)
Standard Deviation (s) = √s²

Coefficient of Variation (CV)

A relative measure of risk/spread expressing standard deviation as a percentage of the mean.

CV = ( Standard Deviation / Mean ) × 100%

Consistency Interpretation Rule

When comparing two datasets or performance series (e.g., two factories, two investments):

Lower CV = Higher Consistency Smaller relative spread around mean → More reliable / Less variable.
Higher CV = Lower Consistency Greater relative variability → Higher risk / Less uniform performance.

Lesson 4 Consistency Exercises

Exercise 4.1 Consistency Comparison

Machine A has a mean production output of 200 units with a standard deviation of 10. Machine B has a mean production output of 500 units with a standard deviation of 20. Which machine exhibits higher consistency?

Step-by-Step Calculation:

  • CV for Machine A: (10 / 200) × 100% = 5.0%
  • CV for Machine B: (20 / 500) × 100% = 4.0%

Conclusion: Machine B has a lower CV (4.0% vs 5.0%), which means Machine B is more consistent in its production output.

Module 5 • Counting Principles

Permutations & Combinations

Master the art of counting possibilities without listing them. Learn the Fundamental Counting Principle, and distinguish when order matters (Permutations) versus when it does not (Combinations).

1. Fundamental Counting Principle

If one task can be performed in m ways and a second independent task can be performed in n ways, then together they can be performed in m × n ways.

Example: Choosing a meal with 3 appetizers and 4 main courses gives 3 × 4 = 12 possible meals.

2. Factorial Notation (n!)

The product of all positive integers from 1 up to n. It is the building block for every counting formula.

5! = 5 × 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 120

n! = n × (n − 1) × (n − 2) … × 1

Convention: 0! = 1 (so formulas work with empty arrangements).

Permutations vs Combinations

Permutation (Order Matters)

Arrangements where the sequence of selection is meaningful (e.g., rankings, codes, seating).

P(n, r) = n! / (n − r)!

Selecting r items from n, in order.

Permutation with Repetition

Arranging n objects where some are identical (e.g., letters of a word).

n! / (n₁! n₂! … nₖ!)

Divide out the repeated identical items.

Combination (Order Doesn't Matter)

Selections where arrangement is irrelevant (e.g., committees, choosing teams).

C(n, r) = n! / (r! (n − r)!)

Property: C(n, r) = C(n, n − r).

Feature Permutation Combination
Key Question How many arrangements? How many selections?
Does order matter? Yes No
Example Order of finishers in a race Members of a committee
Relation P(n, r) = C(n, r) × r!   (every selection can be ordered r! ways)

Lesson 5 Practice Exercises

Exercise 5.1 Permutation

How many different 4-letter codes can be formed from the letters A, B, C, D, E, F using each letter at most once?

  • Order of letters matters (GCT is different from CTG), so use a Permutation.
  • P(6, 4) = 6! / (6 − 4)! = 6! / 2! = 720 / 2 = 360

Answer: 360 different codes.

Exercise 5.2 Combination

A committee of 3 members is to be chosen from a class of 7 students. How many different committees are possible?

  • Members are not ranked, so the order of selection is irrelevant → use a Combination.
  • C(7, 3) = 7! / (3! × 4!) = (7 × 6 × 5) / (3 × 2 × 1) = 210 / 6 = 35

Answer: 35 possible committees.

Exercise 5.3 Permutation with Repetition

How many distinct arrangements can be made from all the letters of the word MISSISSIPPI?

  • MISSISSIPPI has 11 letters: M ×1, I ×4, S ×4, P ×2.
  • 11! / (1! × 4! × 4! × 2!) = 39,916,800 / 1,152 = 34,650

Answer: 34,650 distinct arrangements.
